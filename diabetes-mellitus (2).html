Section 1

What is diabetes mellitus?

Diabetes mellitus is a group of metabolic disorders characterised by chronically elevated blood glucose (hyperglycaemia), resulting from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both.

Insulin is a hormone produced by beta cells in the pancreas. It allows glucose from the bloodstream to enter cells, where it is used for energy. When insulin production or function is impaired, glucose accumulates in the blood instead of reaching the tissues that need it, leading over time to damage in blood vessels, nerves, and organs.

Section 2

Types of diabetes

Diabetes is not a single disease — the three main forms differ in cause, onset, and treatment.

Type 1

Autoimmune

The immune system destroys insulin-producing beta cells. Usually diagnosed in childhood or adolescence. Requires lifelong insulin therapy.

Type 2

Insulin resistance

Cells respond poorly to insulin, and the pancreas gradually cannot keep up with demand. Linked to genetics, weight, and lifestyle. The most common form worldwide.

Gestational

Pregnancy-related

Develops during pregnancy due to hormonal changes that increase insulin resistance. Usually resolves after delivery but raises future Type 2 risk.

Section 3

Causes & risk factors

No single cause explains diabetes; risk emerges from an interplay of genetic susceptibility and environmental triggers.

  • Type 1: genetic predisposition combined with an environmental trigger (often viral) that provokes autoimmune attack on beta cells.
  • Type 2: family history, excess body weight (especially visceral fat), physical inactivity, older age, and certain ethnic backgrounds.
  • Gestational: pregnancy hormones from the placenta that reduce insulin sensitivity, more likely with obesity or a family history of diabetes.
  • Shared contributors: chronic poor diet, sedentary behaviour, and in some cases certain medications or pancreatic conditions.

Section 5

Diagnosis

Diagnosis relies on blood tests that measure glucose levels directly or estimate average glucose over time.

  • Fasting plasma glucose: ≥126 mg/dL after at least 8 hours without food indicates diabetes.
  • HbA1c (glycated haemoglobin): ≥6.5% reflects average blood glucose over the past 2–3 months.
  • Oral glucose tolerance test: blood glucose measured before and after drinking a sugary solution.
  • Random plasma glucose: ≥200 mg/dL with classic symptoms supports a diagnosis at any time of day.
Section 6

Complications

Persistently high blood glucose damages blood vessels and nerves throughout the body over time.

Cardiovascular

Increased risk of heart attack, stroke, and hypertension due to vessel damage.

Nephropathy

Progressive kidney damage that can lead to kidney failure.

Retinopathy

Damage to blood vessels in the retina, a leading cause of adult blindness.

Neuropathy

Nerve damage causing numbness, pain, or loss of sensation, especially in the feet.

Poor wound healing

Reduced circulation and immune function raise infection and amputation risk.

Diabetic ketoacidosis

A dangerous build-up of blood acids, mainly a risk in Type 1 diabetes.

Section 7

Treatment & management

There is no cure, but diabetes is highly manageable with consistent care.

  • Insulin therapy: essential for Type 1, and sometimes needed in advanced Type 2, delivered by injection or pump.
  • Oral medications: such as metformin, improve insulin sensitivity or reduce glucose production, mainly for Type 2.
  • Blood glucose monitoring: regular self-testing or continuous glucose monitors guide day-to-day decisions.
  • Diet & exercise: carbohydrate awareness and regular physical activity improve glucose control.
  • Routine screening: regular eye, kidney, and foot exams catch complications early.
Section 8

Prevention

Type 1 diabetes cannot currently be prevented, but Type 2 and gestational diabetes risk can often be reduced.

  • Maintaining a healthy body weight and waist circumference
  • Regular physical activity — at least 150 minutes of moderate exercise per week
  • A diet rich in whole grains, vegetables, and fibre, with limited refined sugar
  • Routine screening for those with a family history or other risk factors
  • Avoiding tobacco use, which compounds cardiovascular risk
